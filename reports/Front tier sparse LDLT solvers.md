# Speculative pivoting puts smf on the front tier

In 2024–2026, sparse symmetric indefinite solvers fall into two camps, and no single camp is "front tier". **Static-pivoting supernodal codes (MKL/Panua PARDISO, cuDSS) are the fastest.** They never delay a pivot, perturb tiny pivots to ±ε‖A‖, and rely on a couple of refinement steps. The price is backward errors as bad as **3.9e-3**, and inertia reported for a perturbed matrix. **Threshold-pivoting multifrontal codes (MA57, MA97, MUMPS) are the robust reference**, and they pay in delayed pivots and lost BLAS-3 efficiency. **SPRAL SSIDS's a posteriori threshold pivoting (APTP) is the one published design that gets both.** On the hard GHS_indef set, which contains smf's weak cases, it matched PARDISO's factor time, ran up to about **10x faster than MA97**, and kept near machine-precision residuals with exact inertia. Outside pivoting, little else has changed in a decade:
- **Ordering:** AMD and METIS are the same algorithms they were in 2015. The 2025–2026 gains come from engineering around them: AMD-first auto rules, parallel nested dissection (the default in oneMKL 2026), supervariable compression, and reusing the symbolic analysis across IPM iterations.
- **Mixed precision:** fp32 factorization pays only on large, factor-dominated problems, and both theory and practice predict it fails on late-iteration IPM KKT systems.
- **GPUs:** wins require datacenter FP64 and condensed or regularized KKT formulations. An RTX 5080 is a benchmarking baseline, not a production target.

For smf, the highest-value moves, in order, are:
1. Make the matching/scaling/ordering pipeline match HSL_MC80 and test at IPOPT's u = 1e-8.
2. Strip per-factorization overhead from the super-sparse refactor path, where MA27 sets the bar.
3. Replace the threshold kernel with an APTP kernel that has a blocked fallback.
4. Add budgeted, reported static pivoting behind an IR → FGMRES escalation ladder.
5. Make tree parallelism deterministic and tile the top fronts.

A credible benchmark must add **MA57**, the de facto IPOPT standard and the comparator smf is missing. It must also use published test sets, and score accuracy and inertia, not just speed.

## Two pivoting philosophies split the front tier, and APTP bridges them

Robustness starts with the pivot rule, and the solvers differ more here than anywhere else. **Threshold partial pivoting (TPP)** accepts a 1x1 pivot only when |a_qq| ≥ u·max|a_iq|, with u typically 0.01–0.1. This bounds |l_ij| ≤ 1/u, which together with a stable 2x2 solve gives backward stability. Plain Bunch–Kaufman does not have this property ([Hogg & Scott 2013](https://www.numerical.rl.ac.uk/media/people/jennifer-scott/HoggScot2013_TOMS.pdf)). MA97 uses a three-part 2x2 test with Δ = a11·a22 − a21²: |Δ| ≥ ½|a21|², |Δ| ≥ ½|a11||a22|, and |Δ| ≥ ½|a21||a21|. Together these bound L by 4, equivalent to u = 0.25 ([Duff, Hogg & Lopez 2018](https://epubs.stfc.ac.uk/manifestation/40018271/RAL-TR-2018-012.pdf)). TPP parallelizes badly because checking a pivot requires the whole candidate column to be up to date. That forces 1D block-column partitioning, which is why MA86 and MA97 scale worse than Cholesky codes. Cheaper compromises have been measured and found wanting. Restricted pivoting, which searches only the diagonal block, failed the accuracy target on three tough problems even with a matching ordering, so "it does not offer the robustness that we require". Relaxed TPP, which lowers u toward u_min on failure, gave "modest" gains that "do not justify the additional complexity" ([Hogg & Scott 2013](https://www.numerical.rl.ac.uk/media/people/jennifer-scott/HoggScot2013_TOMS.pdf)).

**PARDISO takes the opposite route.** Its supernode Bunch–Kaufman (SBK) restricts 1x1/2x2 pivot search to each supernode's diagonal block and never delays, so fill is unaffected. When no acceptable pivot exists, it sets tiny pivots to ±α with α = ε·‖A‖, and the authors state plainly that "iterative refinement will be needed" ([Schenk & Gärtner 2006](https://etna.ricam.oeaw.ac.at/vol.23.2006/pp158-179.dir/pp158-179.pdf)). In oneMKL the default perturbation for symmetric indefinite matrices is **1e-8** (iparm(10)). Two refinement steps run automatically when perturbations occur, iparm(14) reports how many pivots were perturbed, and iparm(22)/(23) report the inertia of the factored, that is perturbed, matrix ([MKL PARDISO parameters](https://www.irya.unam.mx/computo/sites/manuales/fce12/mkl/mkl_manual/ssr/ssr_pardiso_parameters.htm)). The costs are documented:
- In Gould, Hu and Scott's comparison, PARDISO's scaled residuals reached O(1e-6) on crystk03, and its perturbed-pivot solves ran up to **3x slower than MA57** because of the extra refinement ([Gould, Hu & Scott 2007](https://numerical.rl.ac.uk/media/people/nick-gould/GoulHuScot07_toms.pdf)).
- In the SSIDS study, MKL PARDISO with matching, scaling and default refinement left **stokes128 at a 3.88e-3 residual and cvxqp3 at 8.92e-7**, against 1.70e-11 for SSIDS ([Duff, Hogg & Lopez 2018](https://epubs.stfc.ac.uk/manifestation/40018271/RAL-TR-2018-012.pdf)).

IPOPT's own Pardiso interface does not trust the reported inertia: by default it redoes the symbolic factorization whenever pivots were perturbed ([IPOPT options](https://coin-or.github.io/Ipopt/OPTIONS.html)).

**APTP is the 2018–2020 result that changes the design space.** SSIDS factors 2D tiles speculatively. Each diagonal tile gets a pivoted LDL^T inside a cache-resident inner block, using complete pivoting with the MA97-style 2x2 test, which is proven at least as stable as TPP with u = 0.25. Each off-diagonal tile is solved and then checked for |l| > 1/u. Only the passed prefix of each block column is applied in the Schur updates, and failed entries are restored from backups. Failed columns stay where they are ("fail-in-place"), so no rows move between tiles, and the authors call the backup overhead "generally negligible" ([Duff, Hogg & Lopez 2018](https://epubs.stfc.ac.uk/manifestation/40018271/RAL-TR-2018-012.pdf)). Results on a 28-core Broadwell:

| Matrix (set) | SSIDS | MA86 | MA97 | MKL PARDISO |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PARSEC Ga19As19H42 (easy) | 23.9 s | 29.3 s | 175.4 s | 61.6 s |
| GHS_indef sparsine (easy) | 7.9 s | 9.0 s | 43.9 s | 12.3 s |
| pct20stif (easy, unscaled) | 0.16 s | 1.02 s | 18.88 s | 0.12 s |
| GHS_indef ncvxqp3 (hard) | ≈1.0 s | — | ≈11.0 s | ≈1.2 s |
| GHS_indef ncvxqp7 (hard) | ≈1.6 s | — | ≈19.7 s | ≈3.0 s |

The hard-set values come from a column-aligned reading of the report's Table 7.4 and should be checked against the published SISC version ([Duff, Hogg & Lopez 2018](https://epubs.stfc.ac.uk/manifestation/40018271/RAL-TR-2018-012.pdf)). The weak spot is admitted openly. Columns that still fail after the APTP pass go to a TPP pass that is "not only sequential but is also unblocked", and this dominates run time when failures are numerous. Lowering u from 1e-1 to 1e-3 cut the failed columns on Ga10As10H30 from 1157 to 0, and roughly halved the factor time on ncvxqp7 ([same](https://epubs.stfc.ac.uk/manifestation/40018271/RAL-TR-2018-012.pdf)). Independent IPM evidence agrees. On IPOPT/ACOPF KKT matrices, "SSIDS is able to solve some of the systems that cause all other packages to fail" ([Świrydowicz et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.13909)).

**Delays are mostly a preprocessing problem, not a kernel problem.** Hogg and Scott define "tough" matrices as those with ≥ max(5000, 0.2n) delays under METIS + MC64 + TPP; the set includes cvxqp3, ncvxqp1/3/5/7, bratu3d and cont-201/300. On this set, MC80's pipeline (MC64 matching → compressed graph → METIS_NodeWND with weight-2 super-vertices) at **u = 1e-8 drove delays to 0 or near 0 on every problem**, and one refinement step sufficed. The same pipeline's *predicted* nz(L) and flops were typically **50–100% above METIS**. METIS at u = 0.01, by contrast, produced actual nz(L) more than 50% above its prediction and flops up to 10x above it ([Hogg & Scott 2013](https://www.numerical.rl.ac.uk/media/people/jennifer-scott/HoggScot2013_TOMS.pdf)). Matching orderings hurt on matrices that are not tough: on c-62 and c-64 they gave "significantly more entries in the factor", while MC64 scaling alone removed most delays (same source). On scaling, Duff and Pralet's MA57 study counted failures as follows: no scaling 6, MC30 3, MC77inf 3, MC77one 1, symmetrised MC64 1. They found MC64SYM within 1.17x of the best on every problem and advised that "MC30 scaling is not used on symmetric indefinite problems" ([Duff & Pralet](https://numerical.rl.ac.uk/media/reports/duprRAL2004020.pdf)).

**The robust codes also share a common discipline for safety valves**, and smf should copy it:
- **MA57** has a *delay budget*: static pivoting (CNTL(4)) is suppressed until CNTL(5)·N variables have been delayed, and INFO(35) counts the static pivots ([MA57 spec](https://www.hsl.rl.ac.uk/specs/ma57.pdf)).
- **MUMPS** makes static pivoting (CNTL(4)) and null-pivot detection (ICNTL(24)) mutually exclusive. With null-pivot detection it reports inertia as (positive, negative, null) separately. It treats more than **10% of n delayed pivots** as a sign of bad preprocessing ([MUMPS 5.9.1 guide](https://mumps-solver.org/doc/userguide_5.9.1.pdf)).
- **IPOPT** starts the HSL codes at u = 1e-8 and escalates to 1e-4 on wrong inertia or a large backward error. Its MA97 interface switches on MC64 scaling after more than **0.05·n delays** or a failed refinement, and reuses that scaling until another trigger fires ([IPOPT options](https://coin-or.github.io/Ipopt/OPTIONS.html)).

Every robust code counts perturbations, reports them, and lets the optimizer react. None silently perturbs and claims exact inertia.

## Ordering algorithms stalled in 2015; the gains come from engineering around them

**Serial AMD is unchanged.** SuiteSparse AMD 3.3.4 (July 2025) still runs the 1996 algorithm with aggressive absorption, ignores rows denser than 10·√n, and needs 1.2·nnz(A+A^T) + 9n of workspace ([amd.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DrTimothyAldenDavis/SuiteSparse/dev/AMD/Include/amd.h)). The one new algorithm is **ParAMD (2025)**. It eliminates distance-2 independent sets of near-minimum-degree pivots, giving **3.2–7.3x on 64 threads** at fill ratios of 1.01–1.19. It is *slower* than serial AMD on one thread, and its smallest test matrix has 72K rows ([ParAMD](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.17097v2)). For smf's small-n analyse gap (AMD at about 45% of analyse for n ≤ 4000, per smf's own profiling), ParAMD is therefore irrelevant. The overhead is constant-factor work: building A+A^T, validation passes, workspace allocation and first-touch.

**Production auto rules all run AMD first and call METIS only when the predicted fill is large.**
- **CHOLMOD:** run METIS too if lnz_AMD > 5·nnz(tril A) and flops > 500·lnz_AMD, then keep the sparser result.
- **MA57, indefinite case:** run METIS only if the QAMD-predicted fill exceeds 10·nzl, and keep it only if it is better. Under this rule METIS was called on 36 of 63 problems and rejected in 3. The authors found "little to choose" between the two rules, and concluded that attempts to exploit KKT structure "do not appear to yield any additional benefits".
- **Cost of METIS:** analyse time can exceed factorization time. For bodyy4, QAMD analyse took 0.78 s against 4.04 s for METIS.

All three points are from the same study ([Duff & Scott, RAL-TR-2006-001](https://numerical.rl.ac.uk/media/reports/dsRAL2006001.pdf)). Nested dissection also loses on very sparse irregular graphs: in circuit matrices PARDISO's METIS "generates more fill-ins than minimum degree" ([CKTSO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.14082)).

**Parallel nested dissection is now mainstream on shared memory.** oneMKL 2026.0 made its OpenMP nested dissection (iparm[1]=3) the default. Intel reports it "on average 2x faster than the serial counterpart" and a geomean 1.7x reordering speedup over oneMKL 2024.0, at slightly higher memory ([Intel, PARDISO 2026](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/articles/technical/pardiso-2026-performance-breakthrough.html)). This explains smf's loss on bmwcra_1, where serial METIS dominates. Two domain-specific results point the way:
- **Patch-based ND for meshes (SIGGRAPH 2026):** computes separators on a coarse patch graph, orders the leaves with AMD, and builds the elimination tree during the permutation. It gets **4.58x geomean** faster ordering than METIS and 1.76x end-to-end on MKL. It breaks even only above about 100K vertices on MKL. Swapping leaf METIS for leaf AMD alone gave about 12x faster leaves at +7–11% nnz(L) ([fast-permute](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.00898)).
- **ParMETIS / PT-Scotch:** require MPI ([PETSc MUMPS](https://web.cels.anl.gov/projects/petsc/vault/petsc-3.13/docs/manualpages/Mat/MATSOLVERMUMPS.html)).

So a portable route is a do-it-yourself parallel ND. It computes the top separators with METIS_ComputeVertexSeparator and recurses on the halves with tasks, falling back to serial METIS_NodeND or AMD below a size threshold. This is an inference from how PARDISO and fast-permute are described, not a published smf-like result.

**Compression and reuse matter more than any single ordering.** METIS_NodeND already merges vertices with identical adjacency when `compress` is set, and raises nseps when the compression ratio exceeds 1.5 ([METIS ometis.c](https://git.acem.ece.illinois.edu/lib/METIS/src/branch/main/libmetis/ometis.c)). An explicit O(nnz) supervariable pass before AMD or METIS should cut ordering work by roughly the degrees-of-freedom factor on 6-dof structural matrices such as bmwcra_1 and pwtk (inference). Ordering is the bulk of symbolic cost: **62% (MKL), 81% (Accelerate) and 86% (CHOLMOD)** of symbolic time. Parth's reuse of ND orderings across pattern changes cut symbolic time by a median 2.2–4.9x with fill within ±5% ([Parth](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.04011v2)). Inside IPOPT the pattern is fixed, so analyse runs once per problem. The analyse-vs-MA27 gap therefore matters mainly for short solves and benchmarks of single factorizations. For those, the cheap levers are:
- one arena per analyse, reused across calls;
- skipping redundant cleaning, as MA97 makes checking optional ([HSL_MA97 spec](https://www.hsl.rl.ac.uk/specs/hsl_ma97.pdf));
- avoiding zero-initialized buffers;
- for small n, taking the assembly tree directly from AMD's absorption tree, as MA27 and MA57 do, instead of a separate etree and column-count pass (an inference from how those codes work).

**Amalgamation defaults vary widely and should be tuned with delays in mind.** MA97 merges parent and child when both have fewer than **nemin = 8** eliminations ([HSL_MA97 spec](https://www.hsl.rl.ac.uk/specs/hsl_ma97.pdf)). CHOLMOD uses a tiered rule: merge if ns ≤ 4, or no new zeros, or ns ≤ 16 with zeros < 80%, or ns ≤ 48 with zeros < 10%, or zeros < 5% ([cholmod.h](https://rweb.cla.umn.edu/R/library/Matrix/include/cholmod.h)). For indefinite matrices, bigger fronts give delayed pivots more room to be eliminated, so the rule should not be tuned for BLAS-3 efficiency alone.

There is also a subtle trap in smf's current KKT auto policy. It adopts the compressed matching ordering only when that ordering's predicted flops are within 1.5x of AMD's. Hogg and Scott show that the matching ordering's *prediction* is routinely 50–100% above METIS's. They also show that METIS/AMD predictions under TPP *underestimate* the true cost by up to 10x on tough matrices, because delays inflate the fronts ([Hogg & Scott 2013](https://www.numerical.rl.ac.uk/media/people/jennifer-scott/HoggScot2013_TOMS.pdf)). A predicted-flops gate is therefore biased against the matching ordering on exactly the matrices that need it. The fix is to use feedback from the first factorization: switch when the realized delays exceed about 0.05·n, the IPOPT/MA97 trigger.

## Accuracy is bought with refinement, not lower precision

The theory now gives sharp convergence conditions:
- **Classical iterative refinement (LU-IR)** converges only when κ(A)·u_f ≪ 1, inflated by the growth factor ρ and any factorization perturbation ε. With u = fp64 that means κ ≪ **2e7 for an fp32 factorization** ([GMRES-IR5](https://www.lip6.fr/Theo.Mary/doc/GMRESIR5.pdf)).
- **GMRES-IR uses the factors as a preconditioner and relaxes this.** The general conditions also cover static pivoting, since ε includes the static-pivot threshold τ: LU-IR needs (u_f·ρ + τ)·κ ≪ 1. In the authors' words, "there is an optimal value of τ_s, which is clearly problem dependent" ([Amestoy et al., TOMS 2023](https://www.lip6.fr/Theo.Mary/doc/SparseIR.pdf)).
- **With fp32 factors and an fp64 GMRES and preconditioner**, the stated formulas give a guaranteed backward-error regime of only about κ ≪ 5e7, and a forward-error regime of about 1.4e10. That is our arithmetic on the published Theorem 4.1. Experiments do substantially better than the bounds: the u_p = fp64 variants succeeded up to κ ≈ 1e15 on random dense matrices ([GMRES-IR5](https://www.lip6.fr/Theo.Mary/doc/GMRESIR5.pdf)).

**FGMRES has the best evidence for static-pivoted LDL^T.** On CONT-201/300 with perturbations of 1e-6 to 1e-10, Richardson IR "fails to converge", restarted GMRES "is not robust", and "FGMRES succeeds when these other methods fail" ([Arioli, Duff, Gratton & Pralet](https://numerical.rl.ac.uk/media/reports/adgpRAL2006007.pdf)). The literature gives no count of perturbed pivots that refinement can tolerate, only conditions on their size. A rank-k perturbation should cost Krylov about k extra iterations, which suggests FGMRES absorbs a few to a few tens of perturbed pivots cheaply. This is an inference from standard Krylov theory, not a published measurement.

**Measured mixed-precision gains are real but narrow.** MUMPS with fp32 LU-IR usually gives the lowest run time and **half the memory** of fp64. GMRES-IR converges on every matrix in that set. A badly conditioned matrix either fails to converge or needs so many iterations that it is slower than plain fp64. The best results stack fp32 with block low-rank and static pivoting for **5.6x time and 4.4x memory** reductions on industrial matrices ([Amestoy et al., TOMS 2023](https://www.lip6.fr/Theo.Mary/doc/SparseIR.pdf)). A cross-solver study found the expected 2x "only for the very largest" matrices:
- only about half of 36 matrices exceeded 1.5x in serial PARDISO, falling to 30% on 10 cores;
- **15 of 36 ran slower in fp32 without flush-to-zero**, because cascading fill generated subnormals;
- precision-independent analysis took more than 25% of the time on many medium matrices.

All three points are from [Zounon et al.](https://eprints.maths.manchester.ac.uk/2811/1/paper.pdf).

**IPMs are the hardest case for low precision.** In late iterations, IPOPT KKT diagonals span roughly 1e-21 to 1e+21. Equilibration cannot remove this spread because it reflects which constraints are active. An fp32 factorization perturbs small pivots by about 6e-8·max|a|, which is effectively static pivoting at τ ≈ 6e-8 and destroys the structured ill-conditioning that fp64 elimination preserves (inference). The empirical record fits this:
- **CuClarabel's** fp32 cuDSS mode had to raise static regularization to **3.45e-4** (√ε_32, against 1e-8 in fp64). It gained 1.8–2x on Huber QPs, but was slower or needed more IPM iterations on portfolio QPs, and it warns of failure on ill-conditioned KKT systems ([CuClarabel](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.19027)).
- **Mixed-precision FGMRES** for IPM-like weighted least squares is guaranteed only for κ(D) up to about 1e6–1e9 ([arXiv 2401.03755](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.03755)).
- **No published 2022–2026 result** shows fp32 LDL^T reaching a 1e-10 backward-error gate on late-iteration NLP KKT systems.

**smf therefore needs an escalation ladder that it owns internally, not more refinement steps.** IPOPT's loop runs 1–10 Richardson steps to a residual ratio of 1e-10, then raises the pivot tolerance toward 1e-4 and refactors ([IPOPT options](https://www.coin-or.org/Ipopt/OPTIONS.html)). That would treat an fp32 or static-pivot failure as a pivot-tolerance problem. The ladder should run in this order:
1. Classical IR, stopped on the gated quantities: normwise η ≤ 1e-10 and componentwise ω ≤ 1e-8, using Arioli–Demmel–Duff thresholds for tiny rows.
2. FGMRES(10–20) preconditioned by the same fp64 factors, when IR stagnates (contraction ratio above about 0.5).
3. Refactorization with a tighter u, or in fp64 if an fp32 path exists, following the LAPACK DSGESV fallback model ([gesv_mixed](https://www.mankier.com/3/gesv_mixed)).
4. Only then, a failure report to IPOPT.

Solves cost about three orders of magnitude less than factorizations in well-behaved MUMPS cases ([Amestoy et al.](https://www.lip6.fr/Theo.Mary/doc/SparseIR.pdf)), so on CPU a few extra solves are close to free. The stopping thresholds (0.5 contraction, about 3 steps for fp64 factors) are engineering choices, not sourced constants. An fp32 path is worth building only as an opt-in for large factor-dominated KKT systems in early iterations, with flush-to-zero/denormals-are-zero on and the fallback automatic.

## Tiles beat threaded BLAS at the top of the tree

The proven CPU parallel design has three parts:
- **Subtree-to-thread mapping at the bottom of the tree.**
- **A task DAG over 2D blocks of the large top fronts.** MA87 introduced this, splitting each node into block tasks with DAG dependencies ([Hogg, Reid & Scott 2010](https://www.numerical.rl.ac.uk/media/people/jennifer-scott/HoggReidScot2010_sisc.pdf)). SSIDS extended it to indefinite matrices with APTP on OpenMP tasks ([SSIDS docs](https://www.numerical.rl.ac.uk/spral/doc/v2023.08.02/C/ssids.html)).
- **Preallocated pools for fronts and contribution blocks.** Tacho uses a custom memory pool and re-queues a task that cannot get memory, because tree parallelism thins out near the root and nested intra-front parallelism becomes mandatory ([Tacho](https://www.sandia.gov/research/publications/details/tacho-memory-scalable-task-parallel-sparse-cholesky-factorization-2018-08-03/)).

Generic runtimes such as StarPU and PaRSEC match hand-tuned schedulers on homogeneous CPUs and pay off mainly on CPU+GPU nodes ([PaStiX on runtimes](https://arxiv.com/abs/1405.2636)). This bears directly on smf's thin top-chain weakness and its result that "lean serial engine + BLAS threads" beat its legacy task path. Calling threaded BLAS once per top front puts a barrier between successive chain fronts. 2D tiling lets a child's update tiles feed the parent's tiles without that barrier. APTP's tile tasks parallelize the indefinite pivoting itself. This is the mechanism by which SSIDS's advantage over MA97 "increases with the problem size" ([Duff, Hogg & Lopez 2018](https://epubs.stfc.ac.uk/manifestation/40018271/RAL-TR-2018-012.pdf)).

**Determinism is a feature the front tier now ships deliberately.** MA97 was designed so that results are bit-compatible regardless of thread count ([GAMS IPOPT docs](https://gams.com/53/docs/S_IPOPT.html)). cuDSS 0.7.0 added an explicit `CUDSS_CONFIG_DETERMINISTIC_MODE` ([cuDSS release notes](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cudss/latest/release_notes.html)). An HSL developer warned that non-reproducible parallel runs can send IPOPT to different local optima ([Ipopt list](https://list.coin-or.org/pipermail/ipopt/2012-February/002731.html)). smf's IPM work saw exactly this kind of path sensitivity. The standard fix, inferred from how MA97 is described, combines three steps:
- the parent assembles children's contribution blocks in a fixed child order, not in completion order;
- each task gets a fixed BLAS thread budget;
- updates into the same target tile are applied in a fixed order.

No paper quantifies what this costs.

**Repeated factorization with a fixed pattern is where IPM solvers earn their keep.** cuDSS analysis took about **3.6 s against a 0.04 s numeric factorization** on a 675K KKT matrix ([Pacaud et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.14236v3)). That cost is acceptable only because it is reused, and cuDSS now accepts a user-supplied permutation and elimination tree plus uniform batches ([cuDSS release notes](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cudss/latest/release_notes.html)). On the CPU, the matching lever is a refactor path with no allocations and no index searches: precomputed scatter maps from A's values to front positions, relative-index maps for extend-add, and a fixed arena layout. Delays break these maps, so the fast path should hold while the previous pivot structure still holds (inference). This matters most on super-sparse OPF-like KKT systems. There MA27 beat MA57 per factorization (**1.23 s against 1.9 s** on the 78K-bus K2 system), and more BLAS threads did not help MA57 ([Pacaud et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.14236)). MA27 and MA57 "closely track the virtual best" on PGLib-OPF ([Tasseff et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.08104)). smf's remaining 1.7–2.2x factorization deficit against MA27 on PGLib OPF, per its own IPM notes, is therefore the most common case IPOPT users will hit. That deficit is per-front overhead, not flops.

**The solve phase is memory-bound, and its known levers are batching and layout.** cuDSS solved a 675K condensed OPF system in about 7 ms on an A100, against 52 ms for CHOLMOD, 55 ms for MA86 and 89 ms for PARDISO on 8 EPYC threads ([Pacaud et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.14236v3)). The CPU levers in the literature are:
- **multi-RHS blocking:** synchronization-free triangular solves gave over 2x for one right-hand side and up to an order of magnitude with many ([Liu et al.](https://ntnuopen.ntnu.no/ntnu-xmlui/handle/11250/2494579));
- **supernodal solves that invert the diagonal blocks:** 12.4x over cuSPARSE on V100 ([Yamazaki et al.](https://www.sandia.gov/research/publications/details/performance-portable-supernode-based-sparse-triangular-solver-for-manycore-2020-08-17/)).

For smf, the matching moves are refining several IPOPT right-hand sides as one block, and caching inverted diagonal blocks for small fronts.

**Block low-rank compression is not a priority.** BLR cuts factorization time by about 2.5–4x on large 3D industrial problems at 900 cores ([Mary](https://www-pequan.lip6.fr/~tmary/doc/PHDD16.pdf)), and STRUMPACK's HSS reports up to 7x ([Ghysels et al.](https://simber.simula.no/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/ghysels_lbnl_sparse_rank_structured.pdf)). Optimization KKT fronts are small, and pivoting breaks up the low-rank structure. No study applies BLR to IPM KKT matrices.

## GPUs win only with datacenter FP64 and reformulated KKT systems

**cuDSS is the de facto GPU sparse direct solver**, and its 1.0 release closed several gaps. It added Bunch–Kaufman LDL^T, opt-in FP64 emulation for the factorization, factors downcast to fp32 with fp64 solves, in-place iterative refinement, and pinned-host hybrid memory. These came on top of matching and scaling (0.6), deterministic mode and a Schur-complement mode (0.7), Blackwell sm_120 (0.5), and Windows support from the start ([cuDSS release notes](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cudss/latest/release_notes.html)). The pivoting addition resolves a conflict in the literature: the 2024 MadNLP paper reported that cuDSS's "2x2 pivoting strategy is not supported", which the 1.0 notes supersede. Even so, cuDSS still perturbs small pivots by default: pivot epsilon is 1e-13 in fp64, iterative refinement is off by default ([cuDSS types](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cudss/latest/types.html)), and users cannot set the sign of the perturbation, so inertia is not controlled ([MadNCL](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05885v1)).

**The headline GPU IPM wins all reformulate the KKT system.** On 78484_epigrids, MadNLP with MA27 took **191.1 s**; LiftedKKT + cuDSS took 26.0 s and HyKKT + cuDSS 16.2 s. Both condense to SPD systems so that no numerical pivoting is needed, and they infer inertia via Sylvester's law at a relaxed 1e-6 tolerance ([Pacaud et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.14236v3)). On CUTEst the same methods were "less competitive" and "less robust". The documented failure causes are dense Jacobian columns, slow symbolic analysis, and degenerate problems such as ROCKET, which needed γ = 1e12. MadNCL factors the indefinite K2r system with static pivoting at 1e-10. It is about 6x faster than MadNLP + MA27 on epigrids, but fails 1 of 11 OPF cases, and the authors concede GPU reformulations "cannot achieve the same degree of robustness" as CPU IPMs ([MadNCL](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05885v1)). HyKKT is more than 3x faster than MA57 over an ACOPF series, but the direct solve is "generally more accurate by 2–3 orders of magnitude" ([HyKKT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.03636)). Small problems lose outright. On 1354_pegase, MA27 needed 0.46 s against 1.05 s for cuDSS ([Pacaud et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.14236v3)), and NVIDIA staff place cuDSS's advantage above about 2–3K unknowns ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/why-i-cannot-get-correct-results-from-cudss-solver-for-a-simple-matrix/335506)).

**The RTX 5080 changes the arithmetic.** All of the wins above used A100/H100/B200-class FP64. A consumer Blackwell card has native FP64 far below its INT8 tensor throughput ([arXiv 2511.13778](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.13778v1)); the project works with a figure of about 1/64 of FP32. That puts native-FP64 factorization on the 5080 near a good 16-thread CPU at best (inference). The two escape routes are both unproven for KKT systems:
- **FP64 emulation on INT8 tensor cores (Ozaki scheme).** It is now in cuBLAS and reached 13.2x over native DGEMM on an RTX Pro 6000 Blackwell in favourable cases ([arXiv 2512.08321](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08321v3); [NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/unlocking-tensor-core-performance-with-floating-point-emulation-in-cublas/)). But NVIDIA notes that the required mantissa slices depend on the data, and IPM barrier terms spanning 1e-8 to 1e+8 are the worst case.
- **fp32 factors with fp64 refinement.** This runs into the late-IPM conditioning limits described above.

The right GPU role for smf is therefore **cuDSS as an external baseline on the dev box**, in native, emulated and downcast modes, plus possibly GPU solves. A native GPU factorization should wait for a datacenter target.

## A prioritized roadmap from evidence to code

The ordering below weighs expected payoff, the strength of the published evidence, and implementation cost against smf's measured weaknesses: tens of thousands of delays and 2–8x losses to MKL on cvxqp3/ncvxqp/bratu3d/cont-201; the 1.7–2.2x factorization gap to MA27 on PGLib-OPF; serial-METIS-bound large SPD analysis; and parallel nondeterminism. Two smf constraints also shape it. First, smf's own IPM experiments found that always-on regularized static pivoting cost 4.7x wall time and that a fallback mode broke case9241. Second, opt-in static pivoting with the matching ordering already solved cvxqp3/ncvxqp3/ncvxqp5 accurately. So static pivoting belongs at the bottom of the escalation ladder, not in the default path.

## Credible benchmarking means MA57, published test sets and accuracy gates

**The comparator list is incomplete without MA57.** In the reference IPOPT study, MA57 and MUMPS were "the best performers" on CUTEst. MA27 and MA57 tracked the virtual best on OPF. PARDISO "vastly outperforms" on 2D PDE but fails on a few problems. On 3D PDE, PARDISO and SPRAL were fastest and most robust. On one 3D PDE KKT problem, PARDISO took **65.8 s against 352.1 s for MA86, 749.2 s for MA97 and 2164.7 s for MUMPS 4.10**. MA97 was "generally slower and... less robust" ([Tasseff et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.08104)). That study used 2014 Coin-HSL, MUMPS 4.10 and 2016 SPRAL, so a 2026 rerun with current versions would itself be new evidence. Every comparator is obtainable for academic use:
- **MA27:** free in the Coin-HSL Archive.
- **MA57, MA86, MA97:** free for academics in Coin-HSL Full.
- **SPRAL, MUMPS, CHOLMOD:** open source.
- **MKL PARDISO and cuDSS:** free downloads.
- **Panua PARDISO:** needs a licence request.

Check each licence's benchmark-publication terms, especially NVIDIA's and Panua's ([Ipopt INSTALL](https://www.coin-or.org/Ipopt/INSTALL.html); [cuDSS docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cudss/latest/index.html)).

**Test sets should reuse published lists so that smf lands next to published numbers.** Three tiers make sense:
1. **KKT streams dumped from IPOPT:** CUTEst filtered to the 413 instances with more than 1,000 variables and at least one constraint ([Pacaud et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.14236)); PGLib-OPF up to 78484_epigrids; COPS; and the Maurer–Mittelmann 2D and 3D scalable problems. The 3D problems are the ones that separate the solvers.
2. **Raw matrices:** the Duff–Hogg–Lopez 21 easy and 24 hard indefinite matrices, and GHS_indef ([RAL-TR-2018-012](https://epubs.stfc.ac.uk/manifestation/40018271/RAL-TR-2018-012.pdf)).
3. **A tough subset** defined by Hogg and Scott's ≥ max(5000, 0.2n) delayed-pivot criterion ([Hogg & Scott](http://dx.doi.org/10.1145/2513109.2513113)).

smf's current 20-matrix and 7-model suites are below the literature's floor of about 45 matrices. Matrices that are singular for every solver, such as d_pretok and turon_m, belong in a separate failure-handling table.

**Speed claims must be tied to accuracy and inertia.** Gould et al. set b = A·1, allowed one refinement step unless a solver refines by default, and counted only scaled residuals ≤ 1e-4 as solved. That gate is deliberately loose, to catch gross failures ([Gould, Hu & Scott](https://numerical.rl.ac.uk/media/people/nick-gould/GoulHuScot07_toms.pdf)). Duff, Hogg and Lopez report the scaled residual next to every time ([RAL-TR-2018-012](https://epubs.stfc.ac.uk/manifestation/40018271/RAL-TR-2018-012.pdf)). For smf this means:
- apply one fixed refinement budget to every solver;
- report both the 1e-10 normwise and 1e-8 componentwise gates;
- score correct inertia as a separate pass/fail column;
- count PARDISO's refinement inside its solve time, since its factor time excludes it.

Losing to PARDISO on raw factor time for tough matrices is the expected result for any threshold-pivoting code. The publishable claim is time-to-gated-accuracy with exact inertia.

**Methodology choices are where benchmarks lose credibility.** Dolan–Moré profiles do not rank the non-best solvers among themselves and can reorder when a solver is removed ([Gould & Scott 2016](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/74694)). Tasseff therefore plotted cumulative solved-versus-time with virtual-best and virtual-worst curves ([Tasseff et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.08104)). Pair those plots with a shifted geometric mean and disclose the shift, since no standard exists for sub-second linear-solver timings ([CuClarabel](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.19027)). Report separately:
- analyse, first factorization, warm refactorization and solve;
- full IPM wall time *and* iteration count, because solvers that perturb change the IPM path: 30000_goc took 150 iterations with MA27 against 223 with HyKKT ([Pacaud et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.14236));
- 1-thread tables and calibrated N-thread tables. Tasseff found 16–24 threads optimal on a 36-core node, and Pacaud found 8 best on a 24-core node.

Run every solver at its defaults, apart from documented IPOPT-style u = 1e-8 for the HSL codes. Freeze smf's parameters before the final run, and hold out problems that smf was not tuned on. Both studies explicitly refused per-problem tuning ([Gould et al.](https://numerical.rl.ac.uk/media/people/nick-gould/GoulHuScot07_toms.pdf); [Tasseff et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.08104)). Use repeated runs (Tasseff used 5), and interleave A/B runs on a desktop with background load.

## Conclusion

Most of the speed gap between "fast" and "robust" solvers is the cost of moving pivots, not of checking them. PARDISO's speed comes from never moving them, and its failures come from never checking them. APTP shows that checking after the fact, inside tiles that never exchange rows, costs almost nothing. For smf, the deeper implication is that its delay problem probably lies upstream of the kernel. Hogg and Scott reported near-zero delays with a faithful matching pipeline at u = 1e-8, and smf's predicted-flops gate is biased against that pipeline on the very matrices that need it. Fixing the preprocessing first makes the APTP investment smaller, and makes static pivoting a rare, counted last resort instead of a crutch.

The second implication concerns what "front tier" means for an IPOPT solver. Most IPOPT workloads are super-sparse KKT streams, where the winners are serial, low-overhead codes (MA27, MA57), not parallel or GPU ones. Front-tier status will be decided by per-front overhead on PGLib-OPF and CUTEst, and by bitwise-reproducible IPM trajectories, as much as by 16-thread scaling on bmwcra_1. A 2026 benchmark that pits smf against MA57, MA97, SSIDS, current MKL/Panua PARDISO, MUMPS 5.x and cuDSS on the published hard sets, with accuracy and inertia scored, would be the most current comparison of these solvers inside IPOPT. The newest such study, Tasseff et al. (2019), used 2014–2016 solver versions.
